# AI Builders Digest

## Reader's Briefing

**1. Recursive self-improvement is the thesis to watch.** Richard Socher, one of the most cited researchers in AI, raised $650 million for Recursive to build AI that improves AI, and his book The Eureka Machine arrives September 22 with a counterintuitive diagnosis: science has slowed even as the number of researchers and the money going into it have exploded. His explanation is fragmentation. Fields have split into thousands of subfields, each deep enough to take years to master, and academia punishes ideas that are too far out in front, which is how his own early neural network papers for natural language were treated. His plan is to point AI at AI research first, build it up to what he calls the equivalent of 50,000 PhDs in knowledge and capability, and only then go after physics, chemistry and biology. He is explicit that this is not a hard takeoff: he expects real acceleration, not instant everything.

**2. The Eureka Machine has four pillars, and biology is the prize.** Socher lays out a discovery engine assembled from large language models that absorb human knowledge, a second pillar of scientific measurements that capture what human senses cannot, a third of simulation, and a fourth of robotic process automation that runs real experiments, all coordinated by an agent swarm. His governing rule is that any domain we can simulate and verify gets solved, which is why math and programming change first and why biology is the frontier: we cannot yet simulate a complex human cell. Protein design already shows the pattern, with new proteins that outperform CRISPR Cas9 at gene editing, and he argues that hallucination becomes a feature rather than a bug when the goal is a molecule nobody has seen before.

**3. Personal agents change who the customer is.** Box CEO Aaron Levie argues that personal agents are the ultimate manifestation of "build something that agents want." The form factor he describes is handing off a task and ensuring it gets completed end to end, which requires the agent to operate your tools or its own: your MCP or CLI, easy navigation of your site, and the ability to transact. That creates a new contest for attention, no longer the user's but the agent's, so the tools that let agents order food, handle ecommerce transactions, book flights, work with the local economy and interact with our data will get used most. He calls it the biggest opportunity and shakeup in consumer tech since the App Store.

**4. The people writing checks are being told to get their hands dirty.** FPV Ventures partner Nikunj Kothari says he spends about two hours a day programming, building side projects, tinkering obsessively and trying new repos, and gets roughly a DM a day asking why a venture capitalist is doing this. He is asking whether to open source it.

**5. The agent attack surface is now a mainstream worry.** Aditya Agarwal, a general partner at SPC and co-founder of Bevel Health, writes that after the latest rush of announcements by Google's Gemini, OpenAI and Anthropic about even more agent intrusions, he takes it 100% for granted that a number of major companies have been penetrated by nation states without them knowing about it. It is an assertion rather than a documented finding, and he offers no evidence, but it captures a growing unease: as agents gain permission to act inside enterprise systems, the blast radius grows with them.

The dominant mood is appetite, not fear.** Vercel CEO Guillermo Rauch says the coolest thing about the recent wave of AI is that everyone is excited about building, and that short-term drama aside, people want to create and ship rather than just consume. ## Podcast

### The MAD Podcast with Matt Turck: When AI Improves Itself | Richard Socher (Recursive)

The Takeaway: science has been slowing not because we lack talent or money but because knowledge has splintered into specialties nobody can connect, and Richard Socher's bet is that AI can do for science what calculus did for physics.

Richard Socher is one of the most cited researchers in AI, and he has raised $650 million for Recursive, a company built to make AI that improves AI. His book, The Eureka Machine, starts from a provocation: for all the researchers and funding, progress on the foundational problems, from antibiotics-style cures for viruses and cancer to new physics, has stalled. The reason, he argues, is that we went from a shared body of knowledge to a labyrinth of it, with roughly 34,000 journals that might as well carry no trespassing signs. Each field now takes years to enter, so the generalist who could connect two of them has essentially disappeared. Academia makes it worse by rejecting work that is too novel, which is what happened to the neural networks for language papers he wrote around 2010.

His proposed fix is a discovery engine he calls the Eureka Machine, built on four pillars: large language models that absorb human knowledge, scientific measurements that capture what humans cannot see, simulation, and robotic automation that runs real experiments, all coordinated by an agent swarm. The governing rule is simple. "Anything you can simulate, AI will solve." Math and programming fall first, because they can be simulated and verified; biology is the frontier, because we cannot yet simulate a cell.

He is careful not to promise magic. Biology still needs cells, organoids and huge perturbation datasets before a useful virtual cell exists, and clinical trials still take years no matter how good the model is. But he insists the ceiling is far off: "Boy, are we far away from the true upper bounds of any of the spaces of intelligence." Even hallucination, he says, becomes a feature when the goal is a protein or a molecule that nobody has seen before.
