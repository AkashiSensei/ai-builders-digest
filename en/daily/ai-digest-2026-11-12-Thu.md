## Reader's Briefing

The loudest debate in the feed is about Nvidia's leadership, not a new model. Peter Yang called the cheating at the center of the Jensen Huang controversy appalling but argued that is different from grounds for dismissal; he wants a proper investigation and shareholder involvement before the board acts. Garry Tan, president and CEO of Y Combinator, said ousting Huang would be ill advised because his leadership is central to an engine of future abundance, and warned that a transition could trigger a massive market-cap implosion. Replit CEO Amjad Masad went further, asking who benefits and why now, and suggesting that closed, centralized AI interests, the midterms, or upcoming IPOs could explain the timing.

Aditya Agarwal, general partner at SPC and co-founder of Bevel Health, sees two kinds of AI companies worth building. The first is "diffusion of AI," the vertical SaaS of today: get AI as close as possible to where work happens, whether through an FDE company, a rollup, or a horizontal product. He names Profound, Cognition, and Harvey as examples. The second is the "frontier of AI," where AI accelerates scientific progress and products are built from that progress. His portfolio examples include Externa, Epana Bio, VenomicsAI, and Equivariant. He argues that the second category is underdiscussed and is where the vast majority of model-driven returns will come from.

Infrastructure choices are consolidating in ways that are easy to miss. FPV Ventures partner Nikunj Kothari scraped the subprocessor lists of 803 companies and found that 44% name two or more model providers, up from 33%; that 33% list an inference provider, up from 22%; and that 86 pairs of companies name each other as subprocessors. He said Anthropic grew twice as fast as OpenAI in his sample, that Modal continues to stand out, that Chinese models mostly appear through video rather than text, and that typesafeai is a breakout hit. His caveat matters: subprocessor lists are a lagging indicator, but they are a concrete signal of what AI-native companies actually run.

Every CEO Dan Shipper makes the case that the scarce input is perspective, not generation. On OpenAI's GDPval benchmark, he cites AI beating human experts 12.4% of the time in 2024, 47.6% in 2025, and about 83% in 2026. That aggregate curve looks like replacement. But when Every gave the same customer survey and the same models to different teammates, each person found a different useful insight because each person was responsible for a different part of the business. In one experiment, Claude could reproduce his Slack answer 93% of the time when given 400 attempts, but ranked his actual answer in its top 10% only about 60% of the time. Generation is cheap, ranking is hard, and the responsibility and perspective a person brings are what push past the default answer.

AI is also moving into everyday coordination and creative work, where craft still decides whether it lands. Google Labs launched Tabletop, an experiment for building shared spaces on the fly, with examples such as interactive road-trip maps, dinner-decider wheels, live pickleball brackets, and drag-and-drop seating charts. Claude highlighted artist Mariko Mori using it to write code for a computer-generated animation in her retrospective "All That Shines." And Vercel CEO Guillermo Rauch offered a counterweight to automation hype: wonderful products still require extraordinary human focus, effort, motivation, love, and attention span.

## Podcast

### A New Vision for Human Work After Automation: Dan Shipper, Every

**The Takeaway:** When everyone has the same models, the advantage is not the default answer but the perspective and responsibility a person brings to the work.

Dan Shipper, co-founder and CEO of Every, uses his own week as a test case. Between meetings, he analyzed a customer survey, built a team game, spotted a chance to sell Every to teams, built a CRM, and started reaching out. In pre-AI terms, that is the work of about five people and roughly $200,000. The obvious conclusion is that the model can replace the team. Shipper thinks that reading is incomplete.

He starts with the aggregate view. On OpenAI's GDPval benchmark, he says AI beat human experts 12.4% of the time in 2024, 47.6% in 2025, and about 83% in 2026. That curve looks like human work going to zero. But the aggregate has to freeze people in place. The intimate view asks what a person can do with the model, not what the model can do against a static human baseline.

Every ran a natural experiment with the same survey and models. Shipper found a team-selling opportunity. Yash found that first-time subscribers churn because Every sends too much email. Douglas found that many readers feel lonely because people around them think their AI enthusiasm is strange. Jack turned the results into a Claymation game show. Claude's default answer was accurate but generic, and therefore cheap. "Generation is really cheap, but ranking is hard," Shipper says. A team matters because each person is responsible for a different part of the world, which narrows what they look for and shapes what they judge important.

He extends that into an experiment by Every's head of evals, Mike Taylor. Given 400 attempts, Claude could produce an answer like Shipper's 93% of the time. But when it had to rank its own answers, his actual answer landed in the top 10% only about 60% of the time. A year from now, models will rank better, but the comparison is rigged: it is not 2026 Yash against 2027 Claude, but 2027 Yash using 2027 Claude against the default prompt. That is the allocation economy: as capability spreads, the default answer becomes the baseline, and the alpha is where a person pushes the model with their own perspective and responsibility. ([source](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLuMcoKK9mKgHtW_o9h5sGO2vXrffKHwJL))
